Accurate row detection is crucial for autonomous navigation systems in agricultural applications.
Since the crops are typically planted in evenly spaced, parallel rows, crop rows mathematically
correlate to a specific spectral signature within the frequency domain. In this study, we propose a
robust frequency domain-based image processing pipeline for accurately locating crop rows
within images. Using the two-dimensional discrete Fourier transform (DFT), we identify the
spacing and direction of crop rows from significant frequency components, and their positions
from the phase. To enhance precision, we apply frequency domain interpolation, a Hanning
window, and discrete-time Fourier transform (DTFT) to accurately locate peak responses and
corresponding phases in the spectrum. Extensive experiments were conducted to validate the
accuracy and efficiency of our algorithm. The algorithm accurately detected crop rows in images
and estimated lateral position and heading deviations relative to row centerlines, across different
plant growth stages and illumination conditions. Additionally, by integrating a linear quadratic
Gaussian (LQG) navigation controller with this vision-based crop row detection algorithm, we
successfully enabled a robot to follow crop row centerlines with a mean absolute tracking error
of 3.74 cm. These results highlight the potential of our frequency domain-based approach for
enhancing precision agriculture through robust crop row detection and navigation.
Keywords: agricultural robot navigation; row detection; above-canopy imaging; discrete Fourier
transform; LQG control

1.1 General Background in Agricultural Field Navigation

Robotic techniques have been widely applied in various field operations in agriculture, including
cultivation, spraying, harvesting, and weeding (Åstrand & Baerveldt, 2005). A robust navigation
system is essential for robots to traverse between crop rows without damaging the crops.
Accurate estimation of robot poses relative to crop rows, including both position and heading, is
fundamental for reliable in-field navigation. Currently, most agricultural navigation systems rely
on global navigation satellite system/inertial navigation system (GNSS/INS) or machine vision
systems for in field positioning and heading estimation (Wang et al., 2022).

GNSS/INS can provide centimeter-level positioning accuracy and precise heading estimations in
open fields. However, GNSS/INS-based navigation requires a pre-planned operating trajectory

Vision-based navigation is increasingly applied in in-field robotic applications due to its wide
applicability, low cost, and high level of artificial intelligence. Visual guidance, which keeps a
robot centered in constrained spaces like corridor-like environments, can be effectively applied
to agricultural settings where crop rows act as parallel obstacles (Winterhalter et al., 2021). The
fundamental technique in visual guidance is vision-based crop row detection. Top-view or frontview monocular color cameras are the most widely used vision sensors for crop row detection in
visual guidance applications due to their reliability, affordability, and ability to capture semantic
information (Sivakumar et al., 2021). However, achieving accurate crop row detection is
challenging due to inconsistent and extreme illumination, weed infestation, and missing plants in
crop fields.

1.2 Crop Row Detection Algorithms

53
54
55
56
57
58
59

The crop row detection algorithm must meet the following criteria for visual guidance
applications: 1) accuracy with centimeter-level precision; 2) computational efficiency to
maintain sufficient control rates; 3) capability to detect crop plants that may be smaller than
weeds; and 4) robustness to function under high weed pressure and in the presence of missing
plants within rows (Åstrand & Baerveldt, 2005). Various methods have been reported effective
in identifying crop rows using a monocular camera. Based on the techniques used, these methods
can be broadly categorized as “traditional methods” and “deep learning-based methods”.

1.2.1 Traditional methods

61
62
63

Traditional methods for crop row detection primarily locate crop plants in images and then fit
lines corresponding to crop rows. Color-based segmentation algorithms are often applied,
including color index-based segmentation, thresholding-based segmentation, and learning-based
segmentation (Bai et al., 2023). However, these methods struggle with robustness due to
uncontrolled lighting in the field. To improve robustness, some feature extraction algorithms
were developed and applied, focusing on the morphological differences between crop plants and
other objects like weeds. For instance, Harris corner features were used to determine the 3D
position of crop rows in a binocular vision-based multi-crop row detection algorithm (Zhai et al.,
2016). SUSAN corner features were extracted for corn seedling belt localization for a weed
control robot (Lai et al., 2023). General feature extraction algorithms such as SIFT and FAST
have also been effective in identifying crop rows in the field (Song et al., 2023; Y. Yang et al.,
2023).

After extracting crop plants and features, line detection algorithms are often applied to fit line
segments representing crop rows. The Hough transform is a widely used algorithm for detecting
discontinuous lines in images (Bossu et al., 2006; Choi et al., 2015; Winterhalter et al., 2018),
though it is computationally intensive. Variants like the median point Hough transform (Chen et
al., 2020) and the probabilistic Hough transform (Lin et al., 2020) have been developed to
improve efficiency and adaptability. Least square methods are less computationally intensive and
have been used to fit straight lines as crop rows after semantic segmentation and feature
extraction (Ospina & Noguchi, 2019; Liang et al., 2022). Other effective row detection
algorithms include RANSAC and green pixels accumulation, etc. (García-Santillán et al., 2018;
Li et al., 2020).

In general, traditional algorithms effectively detect crop rows by focusing on spatial information
within the image, such as color, texture, and morphological features representing crop plants.
However, the design and selection of handcrafted features require prior knowledge of the crop
field. Line detection is also susceptible to noise caused by weed infestations and image defects
due to extreme illumination, resulting in limited robustness.

1.2.2 Deep learning-based methods

With the development of artificial intelligence, deep learning techniques have been widely
adopted in agricultural applications. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), which can be
trained to extract abstract features from images, are more robust than traditional algorithms
against variations in crop plant shape, presence of weeds, and illumination inconsistencies in the
field. The YOLO series are representative algorithms for object detection, where the bounding
boxes of crop plants are extracted, and centroids are taken as feature points for crop plants. For
instance, Yang et al. (2023) used the YOLOv5 network to extract centroids of maize and rice
crop plants' bounding boxes, followed by a least square algorithm to find crop rows. Similarly,
Liu et al. (2024) detected pineapple plants in fields using an enhanced YOLOv5 network for crop
row detection. Diao et al. (2023) improved the YOLOv8 network to create the ASPPF-YOLOv8s
network for locating the center of corn plants.

Semantic segmentation is another approach to accurately locating crop plants in images by
labeling all pixels belonging to crop plants. For example, Yang et al. (2023) used the SegNet
network to segment wheat canopy areas for detecting the centerline of wheat, while De Silva et
al. (2024) employed U-Net to identify sugar beet crop regions and used the pixels to find crop
row centerlines. Zhang et al. (2023) proposed the ResC-UNet network to improve maize crop
row segmentation accuracy.

108

Beyond CNNs, Transformer networks have been leveraged to detect sugarcane rows and gaps,
demonstrating better generalization to unseen data (dos Santos Ferreira et al., 2022). Some
studies employed end-to-end CNN models to predict row and column anchors, simplifying the

crop row detection process without pixel-level segmentation or objective detection and
improving adaptability (Quan et al., 2024).

In general, deep learning-based approaches have demonstrated significant superiority in crop
plant detection applications. However, their performance is highly dependent on a high-quality
annotated dataset. Building such a dataset requires substantial resources to collect diverse and
high-quality images and accurately label them, which is both time-consuming and costly (Xiong
et al., 2019). Beyond this, deploying deep learning models on field robots requires substantial
hardware capabilities, which adds to the overall cost and complexity. These factors can hinder
the widespread adoption of deep learning techniques for crop row detection in practical
agricultural settings (S. Zhang et al., 2024).

1.2.3 Frequency domain analysis

120
121
122
123

Most of the aforementioned algorithms identify individual crop rows as separate lines within
images rather than leveraging the global spatial pattern of the crop rows. This can result in false
positive detections, particularly in situations where the plants are large (Winterhalter et al.,
2018), the crop rows are infested with weeds, and there are missing plants (Romeo et al., 2012).

124
125
126
127
128
129
130
131
132
133
134
135
136
137

Given that agronomically planted crop rows are parallel and equally spaced with known spacing,
this global spatial pattern imparts distinct and dominant properties in the 2D frequency domain.
Weed infestations, missing plants, and image defects contribute to the noise at different
frequencies than the crop rows. Frequency-based filtering algorithms have been developed to
suppress the effects of weeds and missing plants for crop row detection (Hague & Tillett, 2001;
Tenhunen et al., 2019). Specifically, the row patterns create a strong spectral signature where the
frequency corresponds to row spacing, the location of such corresponding frequency in the 2D
frequency domain represents direction, and its phase indicates the locations of the crop rows in
the spatial domain. These spectral properties are not only true for straight crop rows but also
valid for curved crop rows as they are largely linear within an image frame. Although there were
studies using 2D Fourier transform and Gabor filter to locate parallel crop rows in aerial images,
they were concerned about how to segment crop plants with pixel level accuracy, which was not
designed to extract navigation lines for navigation applications (Vioix et al., 2002; Bossu et al.,
2009).

138
139
140
141
142
143
144
145

Despite the aforementioned potential in the frequency-based approach in crop row detection,
little research has been conducted on the feasibility of using frequency domain analysis,
particularly the discrete Fourier transform (DFT), for detecting crop rows in agricultural fieldbased navigation applications. In this study, our overall goal was to explore the potential of
applying DFT and frequency domain analysis to address the challenge of crop row detection for
in-field navigation. In addition, with the development of such a frequency information driven
row detection algorithm, as a proof-of-concept, it would be interesting to demonstrate how well
the row detection results can be utilized for robot navigation control in the field. Hence, the

specific objectives of this study were to (1) develop the row detection algorithm in frequency
domain; (2) assess the robustness of the developed row detection algorithm under different
growth stages, field conditions, and outdoor lighting conditions; and (3) evaluate the
performance of a navigation control system utilizing the proposed row detection algorithm.

2.2 Crop Row Detection and In-Row Pose Estimation

228
229
230
231
232

Vision-based row detection can be interpreted as the task of identifying parallel and periodic
patterns in an image, despite the presence of perspective distortion and high-frequency noise. In
this study, we proposed a DFT-based processing pipeline to efficiently extract the direction and
position of crop rows from the frequency domain. The image processing pipeline consists of the
following steps:

233
234
235

1) Image Pre-processing: Correct the lens distortion of the raw image and then transform the
image into a simulated bird’s-eye view using inverse perspective mapping to correct the
perspective distortion.

236
237
238

2) DFT-Based Crop Row Detection: Transform the image into the two-dimensional
frequency domain using fast Fourier transform (FFT) combined with a Hanning window
function.

239
240
241

3) Extraction of Crop Row Position and Orientation: Detect the peak magnitude using
Taylor expansion-based interpolation and calculate the corresponding phase angle with
discrete time Fourier transform (DTFT).

242
243

4) Estimation of Tracking Position and Heading Deviation: Use the row detection results to
estimate the robot's tracking position and heading deviation.

2.2.2 Hanning window for Fourier transform

According to the intrinsic of Fourier transformation, the crop row pattern in the ROI image
corresponds to a peak in the low frequency part of the spectrum. The period (i.e. the inverse of
the frequency) of the peak represents the spacing, the angular location of that peak represents the
direction of the crop rows, and the corresponding phase angle represents the position of the crop
rows. However, directly applying the two-dimensional FFT algorithm to the image and finding
the bin with maximum magnitude in the spectrum is inaccurate in locating the crop rows. This
inaccuracy is due to insufficient resolution for low-frequency components and the spectrum

applied to the image during DFT to reduce spectrum leakage.

The field tests were conducted in the same field and the same passes used during image
acquisition (Figure 3). Using PhenoBot 3.0 as a testing platform, the experiment was carried out
with corn plants at the V3 growth stage during the evening. The actual robot trajectory was
measured using GPS-based localization, and the position and heading deviations were analyzed.
The forward speed was controlled at 0.8 m/s. The sampling rate of the camera for the row
detection algorithm was 3 frames per second, and the update frequency of the LQG controller
was set to 8 Hz.

Our experimental results showed that the trajectory along which the robot navigated closely
matched the reference central path (Figure 10) with a reasonable distribution of the tracking error
(Figure 11). The mean absolute tracking error was 3.74 cm in average, with the position error
being less than 8.5 cm in 90% of the cases (Table 3). Among the four passes in the trajectory,
lower tracking errors were observed when operating in the central region of the field compared
to near the edges, with average RMSEs of 3.22 cm and 5.40 cm, respectively.

As a proof-of-concept, the results have demonstrated that when integrated with an LQG
controller, the proposed row detection algorithm effectively guided the robot to maintain its
position centered between crop rows, validating its applicability for field robot operations. The
navigation control system exhibited robust performance under varying illumination conditions,
including glares and shadows. However, the system's performance degraded when operating near
field edges, primarily due to two factors: uneven terrain that increased the process noise level for
the LQG controller, and increased errors in robot pose estimation.

In summary, the results of this study demonstrated that the proposed DFT-based row detection
algorithm effectively detected crop rows, contributing to agricultural robot navigation in the
field. Compared to other vision-based row detection algorithms, the benefits and limitations of
the proposed DFT-based row detection algorithm are as follows:

523

Benefits:

524
525

1) The algorithm utilizes the parallel property and row spacing of the crop rows as prior
knowledge to accurately locate the crop rows in the images.

526
527

2) By focusing on global frequency features in images, the approach is robust to various local
noise, such as weeds, glares, and shadows.

528
529

3) The computational load for processing each image is low and relatively consistent,
involving only a few configuration parameters.

530

Limitations and potential solutions:

531
532
533

1) The algorithm relies on accurate camera pose estimation for field-based navigation.
Therefore, precise robot-camera calibration, and real-time sensing of robot pose and posture
are required when applying this algorithm in field applications.

534
535
536

2) The algorithm has an RMSE of about 6 cm for lateral position deviation estimation.
Therefore, the algorithm necessitates a controller compatible with noisy input signals, such
as the LQG controller used in this study.

In this paper, we proposed a frequency domain-based crop row detection algorithm for
agricultural robot navigation. Assuming sectionally straight, parallel, and equally-spaced crop
rows, we developed a novel image processing pipeline incorporating Taylor expansion-based
interpolation, Hanning windowing, and DTFT to enhance the accuracy of crop row detection in
the frequency domain. Experiments conducted with corn plants at various growth stages, under
different illumination conditions, and at different positions in the field demonstrated the
algorithm's effectiveness. The results showed that the proposed crop row detection algorithm
accurately estimated lateral position and heading deviations relative to the row center, with
average RMSEs of 6.43 cm and 1.48°, respectively. Using an LQG controller, the robot
maintained a mean absolute tracking error of 3.74 cm, with tracking errors less than 8.5 cm in
90% of cases.

549
550
551
552
553

Our study demonstrated that the DFT and frequency domain method is a viable approach for
crop row detection in vision-based navigation within crop fields. By focusing on the parallel and
equally spaced nature of crop rows, the algorithm proved robust against local noise such as
weeds, glares, and shadows. However, the algorithm's performance depends on accurate robotcamera calibration and real-time sensing of robot pose and posture.

554
555
556
557
558

In summary, the presented algorithm and field-testing results confirmed the feasibility of using
frequency domain analysis for robotic navigation between crop rows, while also highlighting
limitations that warrant further investigation. The framework of the proposed algorithm can be
extended to various agricultural applications involving above-canopy cameras, including
mapping with unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs).
